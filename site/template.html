Method

How the expected vote is computed

  1. Ratio. For each office and party, take the party's midterm vote for the office and divide it by the party's Texas presidential vote two years earlier.
    ratio = midterm vote for the office ÷ presidential vote two years earlier
  2. Pattern. Average that ratio over the 1994–2022 midterms, leaving out the year being estimated, so 2018 and 2022 are never compared with averages that already contain them.
  3. Expected vote.
    expected vote = presidential vote two years earlier × average ratio for the office
  4. President's-party version. The ratio gets a baseline for each office and party, plus one adjustment, shared by all of them, for the party that holds the White House. Both are estimated without the year being estimated.
    expected vote = presidential vote × (baseline + {{b26}} points if the party holds the White House)

What is counted

  • Only Republican and Democratic nominees, and only races with exactly one of each. Third-party and write-in votes are left out.
  • The Supreme Court and Court of Criminal Appeals have staggered six-year terms, so the same seat was not on the 2018 or 2022 ballot. Those years use the average of that court's races with both nominees. The expectation for a court seat is court-wide.
  • The Railroad Commission's seats rotate, so each year is a different seat. Years with several Railroad Commission races use their average.
  • There was no U.S. Senate race in 1998, 2010 or 2022. Comptroller 2010 had no Democratic nominee.

What this measures, and what it does not. The figures show how far each party's vote was from a historical pattern. They do not say why. New and returning voters, voters switching parties, and changes in who is registered can all produce the same totals. Telling them apart takes voter-level and precinct-level data that statewide totals do not contain.

Limitations

  • 2026 is unofficial. The totals are the Secretary of State's count as of {{asof_long}}, with all 254 counties reporting. Late mail ballots, provisional ballots and the state canvass will move them slightly.
  • A short history. Each office has at most eight earlier midterms. The president's-party adjustment rests on five midterms under a Democratic president and three under a Republican one.
  • A moving baseline. Third-party presidential votes vary. Ross Perot took 22% of the Texas vote in 1992, which lowers both 1994 baselines.
  • Straight-ticket voting ended in 2020. House Bill 25 eliminated it effective Sept. 1, 2020 (SOS Election Advisory 2020-29), so 2022 and 2026 were voted under different rules than 1994–2018.
  • Each party against its own baseline. A larger deviation for one party does not translate one for one into the margin.
